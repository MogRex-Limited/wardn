In **role-based access control (RBAC)**, you never give a permission to a person directly. You give permissions to a **role**, and give the role to people.

```text
permissions        roles                      people
─────────────      ───────────────────        ──────
customer:view ┐
loan:create   ├──▶ teller                ◀── bayo, kemi
loan:view     ┘
loan:approve  ───▶ loan_officer          ◀── tolu
everything    ───▶ org_admin             ◀── ada
```

When Kemi joins as a teller, you assign one role and she can do exactly what tellers do. When tellers need a new ability, you change the role once and every teller has it.

## Why it works

- **It matches how organisations think.** People are hired into jobs, and jobs come with duties. A role is a job's duties written down.
- **It scales with people.** A thousand users share a handful of roles. Reviewing access means reviewing a few roles, not a thousand people.
- **It is easy to explain.** "Kemi is a teller" is something an auditor, a manager and a user all understand.

## Habits that keep RBAC healthy

### Check permissions, not roles

The most common RBAC mistake is checking the role name in code:

```go
if user.Role == "admin" || user.Role == "manager" { // which roles may approve? it's scattered
	approveLoan()
}
```

Every new role then means finding and editing every check. Check the **permission** instead, and let the policy say which roles have it:

```go
if w.Can(ctx, user, "loan:approve", branch) { // one question; the policy holds the answer
	approveLoan()
}
```

Now "may loan officers approve?" is answered in one place, the policy, and code never changes when roles do.

### Least privilege

Give each role only what the job needs. A role that is a little too powerful is the usual way a small mistake becomes a large breach. It is easier to add a permission when someone asks than to discover which one was misused.

### Separation of duties

Some actions should need two people. The clerk who creates a payment shouldn't be the one who approves it. RBAC supports this by keeping the two permissions in different roles, and the application rejects approving your own request.

### Watch for role explosion

A role per variation, such as `lagos_teller`, `ibadan_teller` or `acme_admin`, multiplies until nobody knows what each one does. That usually means the model is missing a dimension: not *which job*, but *where*. That is what [roles in a hierarchy](/learn/hierarchy/) add.

## Two extensions most products need

- **Deny rules.** Sometimes you need to take access away, for example a `suspended` role that blocks everything wherever it is assigned. In wardn, a deny always wins over any allow.
- **Custom roles.** Customers' admins eventually ask to create their own roles. That is safe only if nobody can create or grant a role with more power than they hold themselves. wardn enforces this with the [escalation guard](/guides/escalation-guard/).

<Aside>
RBAC is decades old and well studied: NIST published its reference model in the 1990s. Its weakness is not the idea but the way it is usually applied: role checks scattered through code, and one global role per user.
</Aside>
