There are three common ways to model who may do what. Each answers the question from a different angle.

| Model | Access comes from… | Typical question | Classic example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RBAC**, role-based | The **roles** a user holds | *Is Bayo a teller at this branch?* | Staff roles in a bank or a back office |
| **ReBAC**, relationship-based | The user's **relationships** to objects | *Is Bayo an editor of this document, or a member of a team that is?* | Sharing in Google Docs or GitHub |
| **ABAC**, attribute-based | **Attributes** of the user, the object and the request | *Is the loan under the user's limit, during office hours, from a company device?* | Rules that depend on amounts, regions or time |

## RBAC: roles

Permissions are grouped into roles, and users are given roles, often at a place in a hierarchy (see [roles in a hierarchy](/learn/hierarchy/)).

- **Good at:** organisations with jobs and duties; admin screens where people manage access; audits. Easy to explain to non-developers.
- **Struggles with:** per-object sharing. "Share this one document with these three people" doesn't fit a role.

## ReBAC: relationships

Access follows a graph of relationships: *user is a member of team*, *team is an editor of folder*, *folder contains document*. Google described its global system for this, **Zanzibar**, in a 2019 paper. OpenFGA and SpiceDB are open-source systems inspired by it.

- **Good at:** sharing between people and objects, nested groups and folders, very large numbers of objects. Answering "who can see this?" across the graph.
- **Struggles with:** simplicity. You learn a modelling language, run a separate service, and keep its relationship data in sync with your own database.

## ABAC: attributes

A policy evaluates attributes at request time: the user's department or clearance, the object's amount or classification, the time, the network. Policy engines such as OPA (with its Rego language) and Cedar are often used for ABAC.

- **Good at:** rules that depend on values: amount limits, working hours, data residency, regulations.
- **Struggles with:** answering "what can this person do?" without evaluating every policy, and with keeping policies readable as they grow.

## Most real systems mix them

The models aren't rivals. A typical product is RBAC at its core, with a few relationships and a few attribute rules on top:

- A bank: roles per branch (RBAC), the branch tree (a relationship), and *approve up to ₦5m without a second signature* (an attribute).
- A SaaS: workspace roles (RBAC), and *the creator of a project can always edit it* (a relationship).

## How to choose

Start from the questions your product actually asks:

- *"Is this person allowed to do this job here?"* Start with **RBAC with scopes**.
- *"Has this specific object been shared with this person?"*, across millions of objects. Use **ReBAC**.
- *"Does this request meet these business conditions?"* Add **attribute rules**, usually in your own code next to the check, or in a policy engine if there are many.

## Where wardn fits

wardn is **RBAC with scopes**, designed to cover what most products need without a separate service:

- Roles, permissions and a scope tree you define, including per-object scopes when you need them, such as access to a single project.
- Hierarchy through stored links or your own data, which covers the most common relationship: *this belongs to that*.
- Time windows on assignments, the most common attribute rule.

Sharing individual objects between people and groups, across millions of objects, is a different job. Relationship services such as OpenFGA and SpiceDB are built for it, and they work well alongside wardn: wardn for who runs what in the organisation, a relationship service for who can see which document.

Attribute rules such as amount limits are planned as an optional module. Today, check them in your code right after wardn's check.
